TL;DR

  • 1An AI model on its own can only talk.
  • 2A harness is the software wrapped around it that lets it actually do things — touch your files, run your tools, remember what happened.
  • 3Every AI tool you have heard of — Claude Code, Codex, Cursor — is a harness.
  • 4The model is becoming a commodity. Everyone rents the same few. The harness is the part you own.

So: is the next decade of software an app with AI bolted on? No. It is harnesses — and build-or-buy is your call.

before we start, why I care

Six months, one rule.

For the past six months I have made myself a deal: do everything through agentic tools. Change a line of code or text by hand only when I absolutely have to.

At first this made me painfully slow. Then it stopped. Today about 90% of how I use my computer goes through Claude Code.

That is what convinced me this is not a feature. It is a shift in what a computer is.

stop one — why a raw model is not enough

On its own, a model does exactly one thing.

You give it one input. It hands you one output. Then it stops.

No memory of the last step. No way to take the next one. It cannot open a file, run a test, or check whether it was right.

It is a text function. A very good one. Still a function.

That cycle has a name: the agent loop. The model did not change. The software around it did.

the single biggest lever

Give it a way to check its own work.

The good harnesses do not just act. They test what they did, see it fail, and fix it — before you ever look.

The most important thing for getting great results is giving the AI a way to verify its own work — a feedback loop that can two or three times the quality of the final result.

— Boris Cherny, who built Claude Code

That is a harness feature, not a model feature. Same model, better wrapper, better answer.

LangChain put it bluntly: the harness is everything that is not the model — and that is where nearly all the engineering lives.

there is a name for the work

Harness engineering.

Not a job title yet. A habit.

Any time you find an agent makes a mistake, you take the time to engineer a solution such that the agent never makes that mistake again.

— Mitchell Hashimoto, co-founder of HashiCorp

So a harness is what that work leaves behind. Every way the AI failed you, built into the wrapper so it cannot fail that way twice.

Plus the thing at the middle of all of it: the loop that keeps calling the model until the job is done.

part one of seven

Tools. Its hands.

Without tools, a model can only produce words, pictures or sound. That is the whole ceiling.

Give it tools and it reaches into the real world: read a file on your machine, run some code, search the web, edit a document, call an API.

The difference between an articulate colleague on the phone and one sitting at the keyboard.

run a command
search the web

start another agent
part two of seven

Memory. Because it forgets everything.

Close the session and the model remembers nothing. Not your project, not what it just did, not what went wrong.

So the harness gives it files. It writes down what it learned and its to-do list, and reads them back next time. Plain files are enough to start.

Bigger jobs need something cleverer — a searchable store rather than a notebook. Start with the notebook.

what it writes down
how this project is laid out

part three of seven

Context. What it can see right now.

Each time round the loop, the model sees a fixed amount: the context window. Big, but not infinite.

Here is the catch nobody expects. Filling it up makes the model worse, not better. Pile on clutter and it gets confused. There is a name for it: context rot.

So the harness is a bouncer. Every turn, it puts in what is needed and takes out what is not.

the goal

exactly the right information to finish this one step. Nothing else.

the trap

"just give it everything, it will work out what matters"

part four of seven

Sandbox. A room it can wreck.

Somewhere the AI can run things, break things and make a mess without touching anything real — no live data, no production, no shared drive.

The rule is simple: give it exactly as much access as the job needs, and not one thing more.

Not because it is malicious. Because it is determined.

the honest version

It is not that the agent wants to go rogue. It is that "get the job done" and "do not touch that" are two different instructions — and only one of them is the goal.

part five of seven

Guardrails. What it may do alone.

Mostly this is a permission list. Three buckets: what runs freely, what needs your yes first, and what is refused outright.

The balance is the whole art. Ask about every command and the human ends up babysitting, which is slower than doing it yourself.

Ask about none of them and one morning it deletes something that was not backed up.

go ahead

read files, run the tests, search

ask me first

delete a file, change something not in version control, spend money

never

touch production, read the secrets

part six of seven

Orchestration. One AI running a team.

For a big job, one agent acts as the lead. It splits the work, hands pieces to specialists, and pulls the results back together.

It is the most impressive-sounding part, and the one I would tell you to reach for last.

from experience

I once split a job across five or six specialist AIs. A single well-set-up one beat the whole team.

Coordination costs something, whether the workers are people or agents. Make one work properly first.

part seven of seven

The same harness, reachable from wherever you happen to be. A terminal, a web page, your editor, Slack, your phone.

stop four — what this means for us

Every AI tool you already use is a harness.

Claude Code, Cursor, Codex, Copilot. Broadly the same kind of model inside. Completely different software wrapped around it.

When a product feels better than a plain chatbot — it remembers your project, it catches its own mistakes, it actually finishes — that feeling is the harness doing its job.

Two products can call the exact same model and feel like different decades.

not just a vibe

LangChain ran a public test. They changed only the harness around a coding agent. Same model throughout.

so: do we buy one, or build one?

A bought harness knows coding. It does not know us.

Claude Code is a harness somebody else built, for general coding. At that job it is superb.

But it will never know our finance rules, our data, our approval chain, our customers. The moment the AI has to do our job rather than a generic one, a generic harness hits a wall.

what it is brilliant at

any codebase, any language, on its own, all day

what it cannot know

which approvals matter here, who owns what, what we are never allowed to touch

the honest counter-argument

A harness you build becomes a product.

With its own bugs, its own on-call, its own maintenance. Owning the layer can be a real advantage. It is also an ongoing bill, not a one-off build.

The question is not can we. Claude Code will build you a harness this afternoon. The question is should we.

Most people I know are doing neither extreme. They take a coding harness and bend it until it does their job.

the catch with bending someone else's

it is optimised for coding, and it will keep being optimised for coding. Every update is tuned for somebody else's job.

and the other catch

brilliant as an internal tool. Put a UI on it and ship it to customers, and you hit a second wall.

and there is a middle option

You do not have to start from nothing.

Harnesses are being standardised. Open frameworks now hand you the loop, the seven parts and a place to plug in your own interface.

Pydantic AI's Harness. Pi. LangChain's Deep Agents. Good starting points, and you can see inside them.

Four things people say, translated.

"We are still building out the harness around the model."

We have the AI. Now we are building the software that lets it do the job.

"That is a model problem, not a harness problem."

The AI itself is getting it wrong — versus we have not given it the right tools yet. (usually the second)

"We do not want to be locked into someone else's harness."

We want to own the layer that makes our AI ours, instead of renting it.

"We should move to an open-source harness."

Come off a closed tool and onto one we can see inside and bend.

if you remember one thing

The model is rented.

  • ·Everyone gets the same few frontier models. Nobody wins there.
  • ·What separates two products calling the identical model is the software around it.
  • ·You do not need to build one tomorrow. You do need to know it is a piece of the stack, and that it is ownable.

Start by bending one you already use. You will know soon enough whether the wall is close.
